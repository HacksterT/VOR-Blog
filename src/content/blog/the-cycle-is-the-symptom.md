It is eleven at night and you did it again.

I do not need to know what it was. You know. And you know the hour after it, which is the part nobody preaches about — the flat quiet, the taste in your mouth, the arithmetic starting up in your head about how long it had been this time and whether that counts for anything.

Then comes the promise. Never again. You have made it before. You will probably make it tomorrow.

I want to say something to you as a physician before I say anything to you as a Christian, because I have spent almost thirty years watching people fail at treatment plans, and I have learned to be suspicious of a patient who keeps failing the same one.

When a man comes back to my office for the fourth time with the same problem and the same plan has not worked, there are two possibilities. Either he is not doing the plan, or the plan is wrong. Everyone's first instinct, his and mine both, is the first one. He needs more discipline. He needs to want it more. And sometimes that is true.

But I have been doing this long enough to know how often it is the second one. The plan was wrong. We were treating the wrong thing. And every month we spent doubling down on it was a month the actual disease had to itself.

I think most Christians who are stuck in a cycle of sin and shame are being treated for the wrong disease.

### The misdiagnosis

Here is what you almost certainly believe: the problem is that you are not trying hard enough.

So the treatment plan is more effort. More discipline. More accountability. A stricter fence, an earlier alarm, a harder rule. And when that fails — because it does fail — the conclusion is not that the plan was wrong. The conclusion is that you are worse than you thought. So you tighten it again.

That is not repentance. That is a man taking a larger dose of a drug that was never indicated.

Paul asks the Galatian church a question I cannot read without wincing, because I have deserved it. *"Having begun by the Spirit, are you now being perfected by the flesh?"* (Galatians 3:3). He is not asking whether they have stopped believing. He is asking whether they started with God and then quietly took the project back over.

That is the disease. Not the sin you keep returning to. The disease is that you are trying to accomplish in your own strength something that was never yours to accomplish. And the shame cycle you are living in is not the disease at all.

It is the symptom.

### The week you are actually living

Let me describe your week back to you and you tell me if I have it wrong.

Monday you got up early. You prayed. You read. You were patient with someone who did not deserve it. If you had died Monday night you would have liked your chances.

Tuesday was thinner. You slept badly, you skipped the reading, you thought some things you would not want read aloud. Tuesday you would have called it close.

By Wednesday you had overslept, snapped at someone, and done the thing again. Wednesday you would not have liked your chances at all.

Now hear what that means. If your confidence before God rises and falls with the quality of your morning, then you are not trusting the cross. You are trusting the *timing of your good deeds*. You are hoping to die on a Monday.

I have watched people do the identical thing with their health. They do not want to know their real numbers; they want to have eaten well the week before the blood draw. It is not health they are after. It is a good reading. And a good reading has never once healed anybody.

### Root and fruit

Picture the tree the way most of us were taught it, whether anyone said so out loud or not: good works are the roots, and salvation is the fruit that grows out of them. Do enough, become acceptable.

Turn it over. Faith is the root. Salvation is the tree. Good works are the fruit hanging off it.

Fruit is evidence of a living tree. It is not the mechanism that makes the tree alive. You cannot staple apples to a dead trunk and call it an orchard, and you cannot assemble enough good mornings to make yourself a new creation. *"For by grace you have been saved through faith. And this is not your own doing; it is the gift of God, not a result of works, so that no one may boast"* (Ephesians 2:8-9).

I have written before about why the carrot and the stick do not change a physician, and why they do not change you either. This is the same truth from the other side. It is not only that external pressure fails to produce change. It is that the whole framework — the ledger, the scoreboard, the running total — was never the thing God was operating.

### Self-hatred is not humility

Here is the part I most want you to hear, because it is where good people get lost.

When you fail and you turn on yourself — when you spend the next three days in a low-grade self-loathing, denying yourself things you were not asked to give up, running yourself down in your own head — you probably experience that as humility. It feels like taking sin seriously.

It is not humility. It is a payment plan.

You are trying to settle the debt yourself in the only currency you have, which is your own misery. You cannot say it out loud, but underneath it is a calculation: if I suffer enough over this, it will be squared away. If I am hard enough on myself, no one else has to be.

That ledger does not balance. I have said that before and I will keep saying it. The bill was paid, in full, by someone who was not in your debt, *while the ink was still wet* — *"God shows his love for us in that while we were still sinners, Christ died for us"* (Romans 5:8). Adding your own suffering to a bill that is already settled is not devotion. It is an insult to the payment, dressed up as reverence.

Real humility says: I cannot fix this and I am not going to pretend otherwise. Self-hatred says: give me a few more days and I will make it right.

Only one of those is honest.

### Where you actually are

Think of it as three things and not one, because collapsing them is what keeps people in torment.

There is a **door**. That is justification — a legal word, a change of standing, something that happened *to* you and is finished. You did not build the door and you cannot un-build it.

There is a **hallway**. That is sanctification, the long slow business of becoming like Christ, and it takes the rest of your life. Some weeks you move down it well. Some weeks you shuffle. Some weeks you lose ground.

And there is a **room at the end**. Glorification. The day it is finally done, and not by you: *"he who began a good work in you will bring it to completion at the day of Jesus Christ"* (Philippians 1:6).

Now here is the thing that will steady you at eleven at night. **A step backward in the hallway is still a step taken in the hallway.** The door is behind you. Losing ground is not the same as being put back outside, and the enemy's entire strategy is to convince you it is — because a man who thinks he has been thrown out will stop walking altogether.

You are not being asked to earn the hallway. You are being asked to keep facing the right direction in it.

Notice how Paul orders it. *"Walk in a manner worthy of the calling to which you have been called"* (Ephesians 4:1). Not: walk worthily so that you might be called. You were called first. The walking is the response, not the application.

### So what do you do tonight

Not another promise. You have made those and they have a documented failure rate.

Turn. That is all repentance has ever been — not the guilt, not the self-punishment, not the vow. The turn itself. Say the true thing to Him in your own words: *I did it again, I cannot fix this, and I am not going to try to pay for it.* Then get up in the morning and face the right direction.

You will need to do it again. That is not failure. That is the hallway.

The sin knows your address. It has been here before and it will come back. But it does not hold the deed, and it never did.
